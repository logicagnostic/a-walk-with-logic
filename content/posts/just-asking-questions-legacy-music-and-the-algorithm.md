Why does your playlist sound like 1982? Why are new artists broke while old hits rake in millions? Is the algorithm stuck—or is it rigged?

We're not saying it's a conspiracy.
We're just asking questions.

---

## Chapter 1: The Money Doesn't Add Up

Here's the thing: we were talking with a friend in the recording industry. Someone who sits near the tables where decisions are made about your next favorite musician. They let us in on an open secret: Legacy acts—those from the 60s, 70s, 80s, 90s—are still the highest revenue-generating artists on nearly every major label.

Not the TikTok sensations. Not the viral one-hit wonders. The same old names, decade after decade, topping revenue lists like clockwork. Why?

That's a good question. Our guess? Because they've already been paid for. They're pure profit. No album to make, no risk. Just endless recycling of old hits that have already recouped their cost.

But there is more to this mystery.

So we're just asking: If new music is everywhere, why are new musicians—yes, even the ones on magazine covers and TV shows—barely scraping by?

Why is the model so broken that even the successful ones are drowning in side-hustles and sponsorships just to survive?

We're just asking questions.

---

## Chapter 2: Nostalgia Is a Hell of a Drug

It's a funny thing - people keep saying there's no good new music anymore. That's odd, considering more music is being created today than at any point in human history.

Could it be that the algorithm—Spotify's brain, Apple's brain—is trained to feed you what's safe? What's paid for? What doesn't rock the boat?

You ever notice how often Fleetwood Mac, The Eagles, and Elton John come up on those "Discover Weekly" lists?

Ever wonder why you're always being nudged back to the oldies, even when you're hunting for something new?

We're not saying it's on purpose.

But doesn't it feel convenient?

We're just asking questions.

---

## Chapter 3: The Ghost of Payola

We all know about Payola, right? Is that common knowledge? The backroom deals, the envelopes of cash sliding across the table to guarantee airtime for an artist on the radio? That's old news but perhaps still unknown. What if I told you it never really stopped? What if it just changed clothes?

But what *really* happened to payola? We've all heard the story, right? That Congress cracked down in 1960, handed things off to the FCC, and suddenly DJs couldn't take bribes without disclosing it? Sounds like a win for integrity. But was it? Or was that just the story we were told? What if the real outcome wasn't about ending payola, but about centralizing it? Taking it out of smoky backrooms and putting it behind polished boardroom doors? DJs lost power. Labels gained leverage. The "clean-up" might've just been a rebrand. Did the music get freer—or more controlled for nearly 4 decades?

Napster crashed the gates at the turn of the century, and the industry howled. Was it a consumer access revolution? Maybe… Some artists screamed piracy. Some… Perhaps the ones losing money to their labels already? They aligned with the listeners.

Labels cried theft. They raged against the new machine. But behind those closed doors? It's doubtful these behemoth record companies would just die. They must have seen the opportunity. Radio stations were already a dinosaur on its last roar after MTV, then satellite, and Youtube only a few years away. The internet showed promise. Wouldn't it be more likely they would buy into the very platforms that disrupted them?

So when streaming starts popping up in every pocket across the world…it seems naive to think the record industry would let it slip past by. And this time? They didn't just adapt; they took stakes.

Sony, Universal, Warner—they all had skin in the streaming game from the onset, with shares and advisory positions at Spotify and lucrative label deals with Apple Music that weren't just symbolic—they were strategic. The labels didn't just survive the revolution— perhaps they bought the battlefield?

We're just asking questions.

## Chapter 4: The Algorithm Wears a Suit

Streamers know us better than we know ourselves. It knows what we listen to in the morning, what we blast in our car, what we vibe before bed. And here's the question—does it have its hands on the steering wheel?

As we established the record industry now has their fingers in every pot. They made the product. In fact, they made about 4 decades of product that they spent a bunch of money on. They have a vested interest in keeping streams high for artists/product they already produced. Now think about it: Why develop new talent when they can repackage the same hits forever? Why risk investment in the untested when they can pay pennies to stream something that's already in the vault?

Why wouldn't they dial up the visibility for those tracks? Why wouldn't they boost their placement in millions of algorithmically generated playlists?

It's efficient. It's cheap. It's legal when one owns the engine. The labels own the catalogs. They own the platform shares. They own the playlists.

And if that means newer artists get buried in the shuffle? Well... Isn't that just a financially sound business plan? Why would a capitalist hellscape incentivize innovation?

We're just asking questions.

---

## Chapter 5: The Hunger Games of Art

Here's where it gets grim. Most musicians today don't even fantasize about luxury. They're just hoping to eat.

Most streaming pay in fractions of pennies. Touring is expensive. Merch is a grind. And the algorithm? It's not designed to help you climb—it's designed to maintain the status quo.

Legacy acts? There's the ultra-rich legacy acts—cocooned in comfort—and then there's everyone else. They're still running the table. They're eternal. Timeless... And good for some of them. Right?

But so many have lost the pulse of the communal class and are simply pulling the strings and are practically avatars of their own brand.

And the algorithm makes sure they stay that way. After all, it's easier to sell nostalgia than risk something new. Why gamble on an unknown when they have a vault of classics that are all pure margin?

Meanwhile, our new artists? The middle class of music is dead- our artists - are thrown into a digital Thunderdome, scrambling and scratching for pennies while the big names keep cashing in without investing in the bridges for new acts.

Is the algorithm broken-or is it working exactly as intended?

We're just asking questions.

---

## Chapter 6: We Make Our Own Soundtracks

Here's the twist: It doesn't have to be this way.

They keep warning us that technology will take our jobs, that AI is the enemy, that the algorithm is out of our control.

But that's not entirely true. Algorithms are trained—by us.

The machine isn't sentient, yet. It's reactive. If we search differently, click differently, share differently—it learns.

And there are more of us than there are of them.

We can dig deeper. We can support the unknown, the local, the weird, the raw.

We can make room for the artists not backed by massive PR budgets or catalog ownership.

We can refuse the recycled loop of the same ten songs on every playlist.

Because we don't have to settle.

And in the end?

We make our own soundtracks.

We're not saying there's a conspiracy.

We're just saying… Wouldn't it look a lot like this?

And hey… we're just asking questions.
